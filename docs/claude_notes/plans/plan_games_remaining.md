# Plan: the classics still missing, across genres and periods

`done/plan_games.md` (2D) and `done/plan_games3d.md` (3D) went through
video game history genre by genre and are mostly done: see
`CATALOG.md` for what came out of them. This plan is what's left of
both, merged, and the rest of the goal they served: **the classics
covered across genres and across periods**, so that the catalogue
reads as a history of video games, each game there for what it
brought. Their principles still hold (toys not clones, a kit when a
second game needs it, teaching, game culture, performance on the
software backend deciding a 3D toy's size), and so do their
references and their "sources to adapt" tables.

What a game's header lists as "Left undone" (exercises) stays there
and is not repeated here. (Names and dates from memory, to check.)

## What gets in

Three rules, in this order:

1. **Older first.** The weight is on the years that invented the
   genres (before 1995): most proposals below are from there, and the
   order at the end starts with them.
2. **A modern game only if it is a truly modern classic**: a game that
   named or remade a genre (the "soulslike", the "bullet heaven", the
   deckbuilding roguelite), on every best-of list, not a favourite of
   a few years. Each decade since 2000 gets its few, so the history
   does not stop at the PlayStation.
3. **Tiny**: the idea must fit the catalogue's size. Its games are
   104 to 1739 lines, half of them between 309 and 516, the median 417.
   A proposal that needs much more (an open world, a factory, a
   car-physics sport) is "seen but not planned", its one idea left as
   an exercise for a game that has room for it. Each proposal gives a
   size guess, "~400".

## The one to do first: a text adventure

The catalogue's "Action-adventure" section is named after a genre it
has no game of, and its oldest, P0: Colossal Cave Adventure (Will
Crowther, 1976; Don Woods, 1977), then Zork (Tim Anderson, Marc Blank,
Bruce Daniels, Dave Lebling, MIT, 1977-79; Infocom, 1980). The old
plan left them in "Later, or never", as "different enough from the
playground's model to be projects of their own"; the editors typing
text since (TinyCoreWar, TinySokobanEd, the office apps) make that no
longer true. See Adventure below.

## Shoot 'em up

- **TinyCentipede** (P1, ~350; Centipede, Ed Logg and Dona Bailey,
  Atari, 1981): the trackball, a field of mushrooms the centipede
  winds down through, a segment shot becoming a mushroom and splitting
  the centipede in two. Code the Classics' Myriapod is a remake to
  read.
- **TinyTempest** (P1, ~400; Tempest, Dave Theurer, Atari, 1981, the
  vector tube): 2.5D, a well of lanes seen from its mouth, the spinner
  moving you round the rim. The vector games' third, after
  TinyBattlezone and TinyElite, and the only one where the world is a
  closed shape you are on the outside of.
- **TinyTouhou** (P3, ~400; DonPachi, Cave, 1995; Touhou Project,
  ZUN, 1996-): bullet hell, a boss's patterns -- rings, spirals, aimed
  volleys -- and a hitbox of a few pixels, smaller than the ship
  drawn. The patterns are functions of time, beautiful to look at and
  mathematics. It brings the shmup kit's missing half:
  - `Bullets`: a pattern as a function (a ring of n, a spiral turning
    d degrees a shot, a volley aimed at the player); related work,
    BulletML (Kenta Cho, ABA Games);
  - `Waves`: a level as a timeline ("at t = 12 s, 5 of these along
    that path"), data like a `Tilemap`'s strings -- TinyGradius has
    one written out, which would be its second user;
  - `Camera2d` scrolling by itself, the player kept inside the screen.
- **TinyVampireSurvivors** (P5, ~350; Vampire Survivors, poncle,
  2021): a modern classic by rule 2, the game that named the "bullet
  heaven" and was copied by hundreds within a year, and small by
  design. The shmup turned inside out: you only move, the weapons fire
  by themselves, hundreds of enemies walk at you, and a level-up is a
  choice of three (TinyHades' boons). What it teaches is scale: a
  thousand enemies a frame, so a spatial hash for the collisions.
- Seen but not planned: Scramble (Konami, 1981) and Xevious (Namco,
  1983), TinyGradius covers the forced scroll; Star Wars (Atari, 1983),
  TinyTempest's pipeline; Geometry Wars (Bizarre Creations, 2003-05),
  TinyRobotron's twin stick.

## Beat 'em up and fighting

- **TinySmashBros** (P3, ~500; Super Smash Bros., Masahiro Sakurai,
  HAL, 1999): the platform fighter -- four players on platforms, no
  health bar but a percentage that makes each hit throw you farther,
  and a fight won by knocking the others off the screen
  (TinyVirtuaFighter's ring-out, made the whole game). The brawler
  kit's `Frame_data` and `Hitbox` on the platformer kit's movement,
  `Stickman`s for fighters.
- **Two players in TinyFinalFight** (Double Dragon, Technōs, 1987):
  the genre's selling point, co-operation, and hitting your friend.
- **TinyVirtuaFighter's leftovers** (Virtua Fighter 2, 1994; Tekken,
  Namco, 1994): the sidestep (hitboxes with a width), throws, a replay
  camera; `Skeleton` has the poses for them.
- **Rollback** for TinyStreetFighter over the network (GGPO, Tony
  Cannon, 2006): in `plan_networking_teaching.md`.
- Kit: the belt (x, depth, height, drawn back to front) and `Anim` (a
  character as a state machine of frames) are still written inside
  TinyFinalFight; a second beat 'em up moves them to the brawler kit.

## Platform

- **TinyPitfall** (P1, ~350; Pitfall!, David Crane, Activision, 1982):
  255 screens in 4 KB of Atari 2600 cartridge, each screen made from
  an 8-bit LFSR, stepped forward going right and backward going left
  -- the world is a generator, not data (TinyFlappyBird's header cites
  the trick; this is its home). Vines, pits, crocodiles, the 20-minute
  clock. *Racing the Beam* has the chapter.
- **Enemies in TinyMario** (still none): goombas and koopas, the
  stomp, a shell kicked. And the moment for **game feel** to move from
  TinyCeleste into the platformer kit, TinyMario its second user:
  coyote time, the jump buffer, variable jump height, corner
  correction.
- **TinyBraid** (P4, ~400; Braid, Jonathan Blow, 2008): the game that
  started the indie decade (with World of Goo and Spelunky, the same
  year), and rewinding time. The one this project was made for: with
  an immutable model, rewind is a list of past models
  (`gamekits/puzzle`'s `Undo`, at 60 a second), and Elm's time-travel
  debugger was the same idea. Braid's worlds are variations on it --
  objects that ignore the rewind (green), a ring slowing time around
  it, time moving only when you move -- each a rule over that list.
- **The camera's own collision** in TinyMario64 (a camera not going
  through walls, the hardest part, and why Mario 64 made the camera a
  character, Lakitu); `Camera3d` would get it. John Nesky, "50 Game
  Camera Mistakes" (GDC 2014).
- Seen but not planned: Spelunky (Derek Yu, 2008 and 2012), the
  roguelike platformer, a level from a 4 x 4 grid of room templates
  with a path guaranteed -- a classic, but P4 has Braid, and its idea
  can be a TinyRogue exercise; Crash Bandicoot (Naughty Dog, 1996),
  the corridor that avoids Mario 64's camera problem; Alpha Waves
  (Christophe de Dinechin, Infogrames, 1990), called the first 3D
  platformer.

## Arcade and party games

- **TinyQbert** (P1, ~300; Q*bert, Warren Davis and Jeff Lee,
  Gottlieb, 1982): a pyramid of cubes whose tops change colour under
  you, hopping on the diagonals; the isometric kit's third user, and a
  game that is only its projection (the four diagonal keys are the
  projection's two axes).
- **TinyBoulderDash** (P1, ~400; Boulder Dash, Peter Liepa and Chris
  Gray, First Star, 1984): as a real-time game, not only
  `examples/PuzzleScriptBoulders.ml`'s four rules -- the cave scanned
  in one order each tick, which is why a rock falls one way and not the
  other, and the amoeba, the butterflies, the time limit.
- **Arkanoid's power-ups** (Taito, 1986) in TinyBreakout: capsules
  falling from bricks, the laser, the catch, the split ball -- a header
  exercise rather than a game.
- Seen but not planned: TowerFall (Maddy Thorson, 2013), a second
  arena party game beside TinyBoomerangFu -- loved, not yet a classic
  by rule 2; the day one is written, the two share a round-and-score
  layer (`Rounds`?) that half of `games/` writes out by hand.

## Puzzle and board games

- **TinyMinesweeper** (P2, ~250; Minesweeper, Robert Donner and Curt
  Johnson, Microsoft, 1990-92): the third of the Windows games beside
  TinySolitaire and TinyFreeCell. A flood fill opening the zeros, the
  first click never a mine, and the question every player asks -- can
  it always be solved without guessing? -- answered by a small
  constraint solver in the tests.
- **TinyBejeweled** (P3, ~300; Bejeweled, PopCap, 2001, after Puyo
  Puyo, Compile, 1991): the match-3 and the casual game -- swap two
  gems, three in a row vanish, what falls into place matches in turn,
  the *cascade*, the score climbing each link. Tetris clears lines;
  this clears groups and cascades. (Puyo Puyo's two-player garbage as
  the exercise.)
- **TinyWordle** (P5, ~200; Wordle, Josh Wardle, 2021): a modern
  classic by rule 2 in the plainest sense, played by everybody for a
  year, and the smallest proposal here. Six guesses at a five-letter
  word, the colours as the answer; the lesson is the solver in the
  tests, a guess as the one that splits the remaining words best --
  information theory, entropy, in a game everybody played.
- Kit: TinyLemmings' and TinyWorms' terrain bitmap, carved by circles,
  now has its two users: a destructible-terrain kit
  (`gamekits/terrain/`?) is waiting for the day either changes.

## Card games

- **TinySlayTheSpire** (P5, ~500; Slay the Spire, MegaCrit, 2017-19):
  a modern classic by rule 2, the deckbuilding roguelite every card
  game since descends from. The catalogue's only card game with an
  enemy: a deck as your character, a card added after each fight, the
  enemies' *intents* shown in advance so each turn is a puzzle with
  its answer on the table. Three fights and a boss keep it Tiny. On
  `gamekits/cards`? Its cards are not the 52, so only the drawing.
- Seen but not planned: Hearts (Microsoft, 1992), opponents in tricks;
  Balatro (LocalThunk, 2024), poker hands as a roguelite -- too recent
  to tell by rule 2.

## Action-adventure and horror

- **TinyZork or TinyColossalCave** (P0-P1, ~500, text adventures, see
  the top). A world as data -- rooms, exits, objects, flags -- a
  two-word parser ("take lamp", "go north", then Zork's "put the coin
  in the box"), and a rules engine over them; a dozen rooms, a lamp, a
  treasure, a troll. What Zork brought, for "What it brought": the
  **Z-machine** (Infocom, 1979), the game compiled for a portable
  virtual machine so it shipped on every micro of 1980 -- a VM next to
  TinyCoreWar's. The game that is nearly all model and hardly any
  view: the Elm architecture with the picture taken away.
- **TinyManiacMansion** (P2, ~600; Maniac Mansion, Ron Gilbert and
  Gary Winnick, Lucasfilm Games, 1987; The Secret of Monkey Island,
  1990): the text adventure given a picture. SCUMM's verbs (a sentence
  built by clicking: "use key on door"), walkboxes (the floor as
  polygons, a path through them), a script per object; the parser
  becomes a menu, the world model stays.
- **TinyMetalGear** (P2, ~400; Metal Gear, Hideo Kojima, Konami, 1987;
  Metal Gear Solid, 1998): stealth, the genre where you win by not
  being seen. The guards' cones of vision are TinyXCOM's, and the
  guards a state machine (patrol, suspicious, alert, search).
- **TinyMyst** (P2, ~400; Myst, Rand and Robyn Miller, Cyan, 1993): a
  world of pre-rendered nodes, a click to go to the next; in 3D, every
  node a fixed camera -- TinyAloneInTheDark's cuts with the character
  taken out -- and the puzzle as the machines on the island.
- **TinyZeldaOcarina** (P3, ~500; The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of
  Time, Nintendo, 1998): what 3D sword fighting needed,
  **Z-targeting** -- the lock-on, the camera and the controls turning
  around the enemy held, which every 3D action game since uses. One
  room of TinyZelda's dungeon in 3D, and one enemy that blocks.
- **TinyGTA** (P3, ~500; Grand Theft Auto, DMA Design, 1997): the open
  world seen from above, as the first one was -- any car yours,
  traffic and pedestrians walking a road graph, the police's wanted
  level. The top-down racers' `Topdown` on a few city blocks.
- Seen but not planned: Adventure (Warren Robinett, Atari 2600, 1980:
  the first action-adventure, and the first Easter egg); Knight Lore
  (Ultimate, 1984: the isometric flip-screen, things standing on
  things, which the isometric kit's sort does not yet do); Resident
  Evil (Capcom, 1996), TinyAloneInTheDark's line; and the modern
  classics too big for a toy: Shadow of the Colossus (Team Ico, 2005,
  the boss as the level you climb) and Breath of the Wild (Nintendo,
  2017, the "chemistry engine": fire spreading over grass, metal
  drawing lightning -- a good exercise for TinyZelda, as a cellular
  automaton).

## Role-playing and dungeons

- **TinyWizardry** (P1, ~400; Wizardry, Andrew Greenberg and Robert
  Woodhead, Sir-Tech, 1981): a party of six, step by step and turn by
  turn -- TinyDungeonMaster's grid view with its clock taken out, which
  is the difference between the two. (Or Ultima, Richard Garriott,
  1981, the overworld.)
- **TinyDragonQuest** (P2, ~500; Dragon Quest, Yuji Horii, Enix, 1986;
  Final Fantasy, Hironobu Sakaguchi, Square, 1987): the Japanese RPG,
  the lineage the section lacks -- a town, a world map with random
  encounters, and battles in turns chosen from **menus** (Fight,
  Spell, Item, Run), experience and levels. The kit it needs is
  `plan_playground_other.md`'s dialogs and menus, and battle scenes
  (`Scene2d`). (Pokémon, Game Freak, 1996, is the same machinery.)
- **TinyDarkSouls** (P4, ~450; Dark Souls, Hidetaka Miyazaki,
  FromSoftware, 2011): a modern classic by rule 2, the "soulslike"
  named after it, and Tiny as one boss arena. The fight as stamina --
  every swing, roll and block spends it, so the question is always
  when -- the roll's invulnerable frames (TinyHades' dash comes from
  it), a boss's attacks read from their wind-up, and death leaving
  your souls on the floor to fetch back. In 3D, TinyZeldaOcarina's
  lock-on made unforgiving; or top-down in 2D, if that one is not
  written first.
- Dungeon generation beyond Rogue's (BSP, cellular automata caves):
  `plan_teaching_other.md` section 4.
- Seen but not planned: dnd and pedit5 (PLATO, 1975), the first
  dungeon crawls, P0.

## First-person

- **TinyMazeWar** (P0, ~400; Maze War, Steve Colley, Greg Thompson,
  Howard Palmer, NASA Ames, 1973-74): the first first-person shooter,
  and the first networked one -- eyeballs in a maze, a player per
  machine. A game for `plan_networking_teaching.md`:
  TinyDungeonMaster's view and a protocol.
- Kits waiting for a user: `Raycaster` (TinyWolfenstein's DDA, fisheye
  and textures, one game so far); `Fps_controller`, TinyMinecraft's
  player (compare `playground/Character3d`, which may already be it);
  `Voxels`, once a third voxel game says whether TinyMinecraft's hash
  table or TinyTeardown's flat array is the kit.
- Seen but not planned: Superhot (2016), time moving only when you
  move -- one line in `update`, a good exercise for TinyQuake rather
  than a game.

## Flight and space

- **TinyFlightSimulator** (P1, ~500; Flight Simulator, Bruce Artwick,
  subLOGIC, 1979-80; Microsoft's from 1982): lift, drag, stall, a
  runway to land on, the instruments in the `hud`; the `Heightmap`
  kit's third user.
- **TinyKerbal** (P4, ~450; Kerbal Space Program, Felipe Falanghe,
  Squad, 2011-15): a modern classic by rule 2 (the game engineers and
  NASA cite for teaching orbits), Tiny in 2D. A rocket of stages, then
  **orbits**: going sideways fast enough to keep missing the ground,
  the burn at apoapsis, the transfer to the moon. TinySpacewar's
  gravity grown into orbital mechanics, the rocket editor left as an
  exercise.
- A chase camera for TinyComanche3d (the helicopter seen from behind):
  `Camera3d.chase` exists, the game doesn't use it.

## Racing

- **TinyPolePosition** (P1, ~350; Pole Position, Namco, 1982): the
  pseudo-3D road before Out Run, flat and faster, the qualifying lap;
  the `Road` kit's lineage from its start. (Night Driver, Atari, 1976,
  the road as posts in the dark, is its P0 ancestor.)
- **TinyFZero** (P2, ~400; F-Zero, Nintendo, 1990): Mode 7's other
  game, TinyMarioKart's floor, with speed, energy as health and the
  pit lane.
- **TinyHardDrivin** (P2, ~500; Hard Drivin', Atari, 1989): the first
  polygon racer with a physics car -- the loop, the jump, and the
  instant replay after a crash. A car in 3D (the 2D kit's bicycle
  model is enough), and a bank that *pulls* the car rather than only
  tilting the road: what `Track3d` is still waiting for.
- Seen but not planned: Gran Turismo (Polyphony, 1997), Wipeout
  (Psygnosis, 1995), Ridge Racer (Namco, 1993); TrackMania (Nadeo,
  2003-06), racing your own ghost, which is a replay of the inputs --
  an exercise for any racer here.

## Sports and tables

- **TinyTennisForTwo** (P0, ~250; Tennis for Two, William Higinbotham,
  1958, on an oscilloscope): the first video game made to entertain,
  older than Spacewar!, and the side view Pong turned into a top view:
  a ball with gravity and a bounce, a net, a knob for the angle. The
  second user that moves the ball's height (TinySensibleSoccer's z and
  shadow) into `Free_ball`.
- **TinyTrackAndField** (P1, ~300; Track & Field, Konami, 1983):
  hammering two buttons for speed, a third for the jump's angle; the
  sports game of the golden age.
- **TinyLeaderboard** (P2, ~400; Leaderboard, Bruce and Roger Carver,
  Access, 1986): golf's three-click power meter (start, power,
  accuracy), wind, the green's slope; a hole on a `Heightmap`.
- **TinyTonyHawk** (P3, ~400; Tony Hawk's Pro Skater, Neversoft,
  1999): tricks as a combo -- grinds, flips, manuals chaining so the
  score is the length of the chain times its variety, the landing
  judged by the board's angle. Tiny as one half-pipe seen from the
  side.
- Seen but not planned: Rocket League (Psyonix, 2015), a modern
  classic but a 3D car-physics sport, not a toy; Wii Sports (Nintendo,
  2006), the motion controller.

## Strategy and simulation

- **TinyHamurabi** (P0, ~200; The Sumer Game, Mabel Addis and William
  McKay, IBM, 1964; Hamurabi, Doug Dyment, 1968, printed in David
  Ahl's *BASIC Computer Games*): the first simulation game, and the
  first game designed by a woman -- ten years of a city, each typed as
  three numbers (land bought, grain eaten, grain sown), rats and
  plague as dice. Text only: SimCity's ancestor on a teletype.
- **TinyMULE** (P1, ~500; M.U.L.E., Dani Bunten Berry, Ozark
  Softscape, 1983): four players settling a planet, and an economy --
  supply, demand, and the auction where prices are made, walking your
  player up or down the screen to bid.
- **TinyPopulous** (P2, ~450; Populous, Peter Molyneux, Bullfrog,
  1989): the god game -- you don't command the people, you raise and
  lower the land so they can build; isometric, a `Heightmap` edited by
  the mouse, and mana as the clock.
- **TinySims** (P3, ~450; The Sims, Will Wright, Maxis, 2000): a house
  and its people, each with needs (hunger, sleep, fun) and the objects
  advertising what they satisfy -- utility AI, the object-centred
  inverse of every other AI here (`plan_ai_teaching.md`).
- Kit leftovers: a minimap as a layer (a second, zoomed-out camera:
  TinyDefender's scanner, TinyGauntlet2's map), and fog as field of
  view (shadowcasting) rather than a radius.
- Seen but not planned: StarCraft (Blizzard, 1998), TinyWarcraft2's
  line; Rampart (Atari Games, 1990); Factorio (Wube, 2016-20), a
  modern classic, but a factory is big by nature; Into the Breach
  (Subset, 2018), TinyXCOM without the dice.

## Rhythm

- **TinyParappa** (P3, ~300; PaRappa the Rapper, Masaya Matsuura,
  NanaOn-Sha, 1996, the first rhythm game): call and response, the
  teacher's line then yours, judged on the beat but free inside it;
  the rhythm kit's fourth user.
- Seen but not planned: Crypt of the NecroDancer (2015), a roguelike
  moving on the beat -- an exercise for TinyRogue on the rhythm kit.

## Programming games

- **TinyKarel** (P1; Karel the Robot, Richard Pattis, 1981): planned
  in `plan_teaching_languages.md`.
- Seen but not planned: RoboWar (David Harris, 1990) and Robocode
  (2001), robots programmed to fight; Zachtronics' SpaceChem (2011)
  and TIS-100 (2015), programming as a puzzle.

## Order

Older first, a modern classic now and then so each decade is there,
small before big:

1. **TinyZork** (P0-P1, adventure): the genre the catalogue lacks
   most.
2. The oldest cells, each small and each a first: **TinyHamurabi**,
   **TinyTennisForTwo**, **TinyMazeWar** (P0).
3. The golden age's missing classics, a day each: **TinyPitfall**,
   **TinyQbert**, **TinyCentipede**, **TinyTempest**,
   **TinyBoulderDash**, TinyPolePosition, TinyTrackAndField,
   TinyWizardry, TinyMULE, TinyFlightSimulator (P1).
4. A modern one: **TinyWordle** (P5), the smallest proposal here.
5. The 8/16-bit lineages still missing: **TinyDragonQuest**,
   **TinyManiacMansion** (on TinyZork's world model),
   **TinyMetalGear**, TinyMinesweeper, TinyPopulous, TinyFZero,
   TinyLeaderboard, TinyMyst, TinyHardDrivin (P2); TinyMario's
